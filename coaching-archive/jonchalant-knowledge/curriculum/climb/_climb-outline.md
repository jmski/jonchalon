# The Climb — Stage 1 Outline

> Scaffolding for lesson writing. Not lesson copy. Seven lessons across 4-6 weeks. Roughly one lesson per 4-6 days, but pacing is set by the student's honesty rate, not a calendar — voice this in the framing somewhere in lesson 1 or 2.
>
> **Assumptions made (flag if wrong):**
> - Lessons are text-first with embedded video for body-practice demonstrations from lesson 4 onward. Lessons 1-3 use written body-practice instructions plus optional short demo video.
> - The welcome card question (*"What would you have to mourn before you could change?"*) lands before lesson 1 and is the seed that lesson 7 picks up.
> - Coaching sessions are pooled across the full 6 months; The Climb doesn't have a mandatory in-stage session, but the strategy doc notes some clients spend most of their sessions here. Lesson 1 should name this without prescribing it.
> - Lesson 7 ends The Climb. The transition into The Vantage is a separate ritual (dedicated check-in session — strategy doc, §"Stage transition moments"), not a lesson task. So lesson 7 needs to close the stage honestly without trying to do the Vantage's work.

## Lesson 1 — The cost of not knowing

**Headline:** *The cost of not {{knowing}}.*

**Core argument:**
You've been competent enough that the question of whether you're in the right work has stayed manageable. That manageability is itself the cost — it lets you postpone the question indefinitely. The Climb starts here because the inventory only works if you can feel what's at stake in *not* doing it.

**Why this lesson is here, in this position:**
Without this lesson, the next six feel like an academic exercise. Lesson 1 supplies motivational gravity. It also does the work of distinguishing this program from coaching-genre programs by refusing to promise a destination — the student leaves lesson 1 understanding the cost of not knowing, not a vision of what they'll know. The order matters: gravity before tools, not the reverse.

**Embodied component:**
Twice a day for five days — once in the morning before work, once in the evening after — sit still for two minutes and answer one question in writing: *Where in my body am I right now?* Use any vocabulary that comes (tight, light, heavy, blank, fizzing, locked). No interpretation. No goal of changing it. The instruction is to track, not to fix. By day five the student should have ten short entries that map onto the working day. The point is not the entries; the point is becoming someone who notices.

**Reflection prompt seed:**
*What has staying competent been costing you that you've stopped counting?*

(This question is meant to surface the things that have been re-framed as acceptable trade-offs — relationships deprioritized, hobbies dropped, body ignored. Not a list of complaints. A list of trades.)

**Voice notes:**
The temptation here is to motivate. Don't. Register A throughout. The lesson is not *"imagine what you could become"* — it's *"look honestly at what you've been quietly paying."* Banned-word risk: *unlock*, *transform*, *journey* will all want to appear in the framing. The opening sentence cannot promise an outcome. If the lesson reads like an invitation, rewrite it as a statement.

## Lesson 2 — Honest self-assessment

**Headline:** *Honest self-{{assessment}}.*

**Core argument:**
Most self-assessment is a performance for an imagined audience — a future employer, a partner, your past self, your own sense of being a good person. Honest self-assessment is the practice of noticing when you're performing and stopping. It's a skill, not a moment of clarity. This lesson teaches the difference and gives you a baseline.

**Why this lesson is here, in this position:**
Lesson 1 supplies the gravity; lesson 2 supplies the diagnostic stance. You can't catalogue what you do (lesson 5) until you can tell the difference between what you do and what you tell yourself you do. The two-position order — gravity first, then stance — also means the student isn't being asked to be honest *to* anyone yet. They're being asked to notice when they're not being honest with themselves. That's a smaller, more accessible first move than full inventory.

**Embodied component:**
One exercise, done once, for thirty to forty-five minutes in a single sitting. Write three answers to one question — *What am I good at?* — under three explicit framings, in this order:
1. *What would I say to a recruiter?* (the public résumé voice)
2. *What would I say to a friend who already likes me?* (the warm-mirror voice)
3. *What would I say if no one would ever read this?* (the no-audience voice)

Notice — physically — what each version feels like in the body as you write it. The first usually tightens the chest or jaw. The second softens. The third can produce something that looks small on the page and feels disproportionately exposing. The exercise is not the three answers. The exercise is feeling the three voices as three different body-states.

**Reflection prompt seed:**
*Which of the three versions was hardest to write, and what does that tell you about who you've been writing for?*

**Voice notes:**
Don't soften the third framing into *"speaking from your authentic self."* That phrase is banned for a reason — it implies a stable hidden truth waiting to be uncovered, when what's actually happening is the absence of an audience. The third voice is not deeper. It's just unwitnessed. Stay precise. Register A.

## Lesson 3 — The body as data source

**Headline:** *The body as {{data}} source.*

**Core argument:**
Your body has been registering information about your work, your relationships, and your alignment for years — most of it more accurate than what you've been telling yourself. This lesson makes the claim explicitly and gives the student a way to start reading their own body the way a coach would. It is not about feelings. It is about information.

**Why this lesson is here, in this position:**
This is the last lesson before the student picks a medium (lesson 4). The position is structural: lesson 3 has to establish that the body carries information *in general* before lesson 4 asks the student to commit to a specific physical practice for the next six months. If the embodied epistemology isn't claimed here, the medium choice in lesson 4 reads as a fitness recommendation. Lessons 1 and 2 are also slightly more cerebral — lesson 3 is where the body comes forward and stays forward.

**Embodied component:**
A four-state scan, three times a day for one week. The student names where their body is on a four-point scale and notes what they were doing in the moment when they checked. Four states (proposed — refine if these don't feel right):
- **Open** — chest forward, breath full, attention outward, willing
- **Neutral** — functional, present, no strong pull either direction
- **Closed** — shoulders forward, breath shallow, attention narrowed, wanting to leave
- **Contracted** — held, jaw tight, gut clenched, in withdrawal or readiness for impact

By the end of the week the student has ~21 data points mapped to context (in meeting with X, writing report Y, talking to Z, alone). The pattern is the lesson. The vocabulary is the secondary lesson — they now have words their body understands.

**Reflection prompt seed:**
*Which contexts moved your body toward closed or contracted most reliably? What did you tell yourself about those contexts at the time?*

**Voice notes:**
Two real risks here. (1) This is where the brand could slide into wellness-genre — *"learn to listen to your body"* type lines. Don't. The frame is information, not wisdom. The body is a sensor, not an oracle. (2) The four-state vocabulary is a proposal in this outline, not a lock. If Jon has a different scale he's been using in his own practice, that one wins. The shape of the lesson (a repeated body-scan with named states across a week, mapped to context) is the locked structure. The specific words are open.

## Lesson 4 — Finding your physical medium

**Headline:** *Finding your physical {{medium}}.*

**Core argument:**
The Foundation is not a dance program. Every Foundation client picks one physical medium and stays with it for the duration of the engagement. This lesson is where the student chooses theirs. The choice is structural — the medium is the lab where every subsequent lesson will get tested.

**Why this lesson is here, in this position:**
This is the pivot point of the stage. Lessons 1-3 build the capacity to take this choice seriously; lessons 5-7 require a medium to land in. If the medium is picked earlier than lesson 4, the student hasn't yet claimed the body as a data source (lesson 3) and the choice becomes either a fitness goal or a status performance. If it's picked later, the inventory work in lesson 5 has no embodied counterpart.

**Embodied component:**
Three-part exercise, completed across roughly a week.

1. **The criteria check.** The student writes down candidate mediums (anywhere from 2 to 7) and runs each one against four criteria: *does it require presence, does it give feedback, can I sustain it for years, am I doing it for status?* The fourth is the disqualifier. The first three are the qualifiers. Most candidates fail on at least one.

2. **The three-session trial.** The student takes the top one or two candidates and does three real sessions of each — not classes they're observing, sessions they're inside. Each session ends with a one-line body-state note using the lesson-3 vocabulary (or whatever vocabulary lesson 3 settled on).

3. **The commitment.** At the end of the week the student commits to one medium in writing and tells Jon (via the weekly check-in). The commitment is for the duration of the Foundation engagement — six months minimum. The reason for the formal commitment is to make changing mediums effortful, so that boredom or difficulty in month two doesn't get re-framed as *"this wasn't the right one."*

**Reflection prompt seed:**
*Which candidate made your body do something you weren't expecting — and how did you explain that to yourself afterward?*

**Voice notes:**
The four-criteria filter is the load-bearing thing — write it cleanly enough that someone could quote it back. The *not doing it for status* criterion is the one most likely to trip the student; expect resistance. Don't soften it. The bullshit clause has a place here: if the student is choosing a medium because of how it will sound to other people, the lesson should be willing to call that. Used once, sparingly. Don't make the whole lesson a confrontation — the gravity is in the commitment, not in the calling-out.

## Lesson 5 — Cataloguing what you do

**Headline:** *Cataloguing what you {{do}}.*

**Core argument:**
You're going to inventory what your actual life contains across the four ikigai circles — Passion, Mission, Vocation, Profession — and the rule is that nothing counts if you haven't done it in the last twelve months. This is not aspirational. This is not historical. This is what your life *currently* contains.

**Why this lesson is here, in this position:**
This is the central act of The Climb. The previous four lessons build the capacity (gravity, stance, body-data, medium); the next two metabolize what the catalogue reveals. Lesson 5 is where the actual inventory happens. The strategy doc and Foundation page both promise: *"most clients find the inventory itself takes a week — the honesty takes longer."* This lesson is the inventory week. Lessons 6 and 7 are where the honesty extends.

**Embodied component:**
The catalogue is done in two passes, with body-checking between them.

**Pass 1 — fast list.** Single sitting, 45 minutes maximum. Four columns on a page or screen: Passion (what you love), Mission (what the world needs that you'd give), Vocation (what you can be paid for), Profession (what you're trained or qualified to do). Fill each column with whatever comes to mind. Twelve-month rule applies: if you haven't done it in the last twelve months, it's not on the list. Speed is the point. No editing.

**Body check.** Read the four columns aloud, one at a time. After each, do a 30-second body-state check using the lesson-3 vocabulary. The student now has four body-state readings, one per circle. The contraction or closure on a specific circle is data.

**Pass 2 — second sitting, 24-48 hours later.** Now in your chosen medium. Move (dance, walk a trail, lift, swim, train, whatever) for at least twenty minutes *before* opening the catalogue. Then re-read each column and edit. Items that were on the list out of obligation tend to feel different after movement. Items that were missing tend to surface. The student is not adding aspirational items — they're correcting the first pass against what the body produced after movement.

**Reflection prompt seed:**
*Which column was the hardest to fill, and which was the hardest to keep honest?*

**Voice notes:**
The temptation is to introduce nuance — *"don't worry if your lists feel uneven."* Don't. The point of the lesson is that the lists *will* be uneven, and the unevenness is the diagnostic. Resist the urge to make this feel safe. Register A throughout. One soft Register B beat at the end is appropriate — the lesson does ask something hard.

Also: the twelve-month rule is the load-bearing constraint. Without it the inventory drifts into résumé territory. Defend the rule explicitly in the lesson copy.

## Lesson 6 — The circles you avoid

**Headline:** *The circles you {{avoid}}.*

**Core argument:**
The Climb's diagnostic is not what's full. It's what's missing. You'll look at your inventory from lesson 5 and identify the one circle that's structurally thin — the one you've been avoiding. Most clients land on Mission or Passion. Some find it's Vocation in disguise. The point is to name it specifically and not move on until you can say it without flinching.

**Why this lesson is here, in this position:**
This lesson is the *diagnostic conclusion* of The Climb. It needs to come after the inventory (lesson 5) because you can't name what's missing until you've honestly catalogued what's there. It needs to come before the mourning lesson (lesson 7) because what gets named here is what gets mourned next. The naming is the bridge.

**Embodied component:**
The student returns to the four columns from lesson 5 and ranks them — first instinct, no overthinking — from fullest to thinnest. The thinnest one is the working hypothesis.

Then, in their chosen medium, the student does a 30-minute session with one instruction: *move as if you already had the thing the thinnest column needs.* The instruction is deliberately vague. If the thinnest column is Mission, move as if you already knew what you were for. If it's Passion, move as if you already had something you loved. If it's Vocation, move as if your work were already legible. The student is not being asked to figure out what the thing is. They're being asked to feel the absence of it by simulating its presence.

After the session, two body-state readings: one taken at the session's start (recall, written down), one taken at the end. The difference is the data. If the absence feels like absence — if simulating its presence opens or quiets the body — the working hypothesis is probably right. If nothing moved, try the second-thinnest column with the same instruction the next day.

**Reflection prompt seed:**
*Name your missing circle out loud, to yourself, in one short sentence. What did saying it cost?*

**Voice notes:**
Two risks. (1) The lesson must not slide into the language of *discovery*. The student is not discovering a hidden circle; they're naming what their honest inventory has already shown. The word *reveal* should not appear. (2) Some students will fight the naming. The lesson should anticipate this without diagnosing it — Register A on the naming, Register B on the resistance. *"Most people don't want to name it. That's the point of naming it."* That kind of line is the right register.

The simulation exercise is the load-bearing move. If something in the writing process suggests a simpler version, take it — but the body has to be the instrument of the conclusion, not the journal.

## Lesson 7 — Mourning what isn't working

**Headline:** *Mourning what isn't {{working}}.*

**Core argument:**
You can't grow a missing circle while still holding the dead pieces of the old life as live commitments. The Climb ends with a piece of grief work — naming what isn't working, and letting it be dead so it can stop running your week. This is not a closure ritual. It's a structural move that clears the load before The Vantage.

**Why this lesson is here, in this position:**
This is the closing move of the stage. The welcome card question — *"What would you have to mourn before you could change?"* — was seeded on day one and waits here. Lessons 1-6 build the capacity and do the diagnostic; lesson 7 metabolizes the cost. Putting it last is correct: you cannot mourn what you have not yet named (lesson 6), and you cannot enter active integration (The Vantage) while still defending what's already dead. The position is non-negotiable. The risk of ending the stage on grief is real — see voice notes.

**Embodied component:**
Two parts, done across the final 5-7 days of The Climb.

**Part 1 — the list.** Single sitting. The student writes a list of things to mourn. Not regrets. Not failures. Things they were doing or being or maintaining that the inventory revealed are dead — roles they've outgrown, projects they're maintaining out of obligation, identities they've been performing, relationships that haven't been alive for years, ambitions that were someone else's. Twelve to twenty items is normal. Some clients land at four; some at thirty. The list is private.

**Part 2 — the body practice.** In the chosen medium, three sessions across one week. Each session, the student picks one item from the list and brings it into the practice — not as a story to work through, but as something to hold in the body while moving. The instruction is plain: *move with this item present, until something shifts.* The shift may be small (a release in the chest, a piece of the item that suddenly feels lighter). It may not come. If it doesn't, the student notes that and picks a different item next session. By the end of the week, three items have been brought into the body. The remaining items wait. Some of them will mourn themselves over the next months. Some will need help. The lesson is to begin, not to finish.

**Reflection prompt seed:**
*Which item, if you let it actually be dead, would change how next week feels?*

(This prompt is the small forward turn — see voice notes.)

**Voice notes:**
The biggest risk in the stage is here. Mourning is real work and the lesson must not flinch from it — but ending The Climb on flatness or despair sets up The Vantage badly. Two structural moves protect against this without compromising the grief work:

1. The reflection prompt asks a forward-leaning question. Not *"what are you mourning?"* (which lesson 7 has already done) but *"which one, if released, changes next week?"* The forward turn is small and specific, not motivational.

2. The lesson should close with one Register B beat — short, warm, present — acknowledging that the student has just finished a stage. Not congratulations. Acknowledgment. *"You've finished The Climb. The next session with me is for naming what shifted. Bring the list."* Something in that register. The transition session (strategy doc, §"Stage transition moments") does the rest.

Banned-word risk is high. *Release* will want to appear; use sparingly. *Closure* should never appear — closure is not what mourning produces. *Let go* is acceptable once, maybe twice. *Healing* should not appear at all.

The lesson should not promise that mourning the items will feel good. It should promise that not mourning them is what's making next week heavy.

## Arc check

The seven lessons move from *why is this worth doing* (1) → *how do I tell honest from performed* (2) → *what does my body know that my mind hasn't admitted* (3) → *commit to one lab for the next six months* (4) → *do the actual inventory* (5) → *name what's missing* (6) → *let go of what's dead* (7). The first three are pre-medium and build capacity; lesson 4 is the structural pivot; the last three are post-medium and do the work that capacity makes possible. Felt experience: lessons 1-2 produce a low-grade unease — the student feels they've been caught, accurately. Lesson 3 is the first real opening, when the body becomes legible as information. Lesson 4 produces a clean commitment that should feel sober rather than excited. Lesson 5 is the heaviest cognitively — the inventory exposes a lot, fast — and lesson 6 lands the diagnostic, which usually arrives as a quiet recognition rather than a revelation. Lesson 7 closes the stage on real grief, with a small forward turn that hands the student to the Vantage's transition session without false uplift. The arc earns its name: the climb is exactly what this is — incrementally harder air, a longer view at the top, and the cost of having moved.

## Gaps and flags for the writing phase

These are things I noticed while outlining that aren't lessons but need to exist somewhere before the writing starts. None should change the lesson count.

1. **Lesson 3's body-state vocabulary** (open / neutral / closed / contracted) is a proposal, not a lock. If Jon has a different scale from his own dance practice, that wins. The lesson structure stays; the words are open.

2. **The twelve-month rule** in lesson 5 is the load-bearing diagnostic constraint and probably deserves a one-line statement on the Foundation page or the Climb module page, not just inside the lesson. Without it, the inventory drifts into résumé territory.

3. **Coaching contact during The Climb** isn't structurally fixed — sessions are pooled. The lessons themselves should not assume a session has happened or is about to. But the canonical-copy framing already promises that *some clients spend most of their sessions in The Climb*. Lesson 1 or lesson 6 is the right place to make the offer visible: *"if the inventory is breaking you, book a session — that's what they're for."* One sentence, no urgency.

4. **The welcome card question** (*"What would you have to mourn before you could change?"*) is currently seeded only in the founding-cohort experience, but its payload lands in lesson 7. The lesson should not reference the card explicitly (lesson 7 needs to work for standalone module buyers too, who don't get a welcome card), but the writing should leave a clean fit for cohort members who *do* have the question already in hand.

5. **The transition to The Vantage** is a session, not a lesson, per the strategy doc. Lesson 7 should hand off to it cleanly without doing its work. The line in lesson 7's voice notes — *"the next session with me is for naming what shifted"* — is the right scope. Anything more belongs in The Vantage's lesson 1.
